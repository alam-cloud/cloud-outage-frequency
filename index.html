02

The vocabulary: SLI → SLO → error budget → SLA

Every number on this page is an error-budget number. Here's the chain that turns an incident into a burnt budget — and where the trap is that most teams fall into.

SLI

The measurement

A precisely defined, quantitative measure of one aspect of your service. Not "uptime" — a good SLI counts what users actually experience.

good: proportion of valid requests served in <400ms
SLO

The target

A target value or range for an SLI, measured over a stated window. This is the number you set and the number you report to your own engineers.

99.9% of valid requests over 28 days
BUDGET

The permission to fail

(1 − SLO) × window is how much failure you're allowed to buy. Spending it is a deliberate engineering choice, not a failure.

99.9% × 28 days = 40.3 min of allowed failure
SLA

The contract

An external promise to a customer, with financial consequences when broken. The provider's SLA is not your SLO — and your SLA is never your provider's.

AWS EC2 multi-AZ: 99.99%, credits 10–100%

Alerting on SLOs: multi-window, multi-burn-rate

Burn rate is how fast you're consuming the budget relative to the sustainable rate. 1× means you'd exactly exhaust the budget at the end of the window. The Google SRE Workbook's standard approach pairs a long window (enough budget has really been spent to justify waking someone) with a short window (so the alert stops minutes after the problem is fixed, not hours later). An alert fires only when both windows breach.

The trap: your SLA is not your SLO, and your provider's SLA is not yours

A stack of ALB (99.99%) × EC2 multi-AZ (99.99%) × RDS Multi-AZ (99.95%) × S3 (99.9%) composes to ~99.83% — about 73 minutes of expected dependency downtime per month. That's worse than the 99.9% you're likely about to promise a customer, and the gap is invisible if you only ever read the headline SLA of each component.

Meanwhile the provider's SLA remedy is a percentage of your bill. In the October 2025 AWS event a 15-hour outage produced a $1,500 credit on a $5,000/month EC2 bill, against roughly $27.7M of business impact at the $1.85M/hour rate New Relic reports for high-impact outages. SLA credits are a service gesture, not insurance.

04

Anatomy of a failure: AWS us-east-1, October 2025

The single most instructive outage in the dataset — because it failed in the most instructive way: not hardware, not capacity, but a race condition in the automation that manages DNS. It took 14 hours 32 minutes to fully resolve, and AWS's own engineers needed to intervene by hand.

How eight unlikely things became certain

  1. Enactor 1 starts applying a DNS planIt hits significant lock contention and takes far longer than usual.
  2. The Planner keeps generating newer plansIt doesn't know Enactor 1 is still working, so the plan Enactor 1 is applying is now obsolete.
  3. Enactor 2 applies a newer, valid planThe regional endpoint's DNS records are correct again.
  4. Enactor 1 finishes and overwrites themThe slow one lands last, clobbering the good records with its outdated plan.
  5. Enactor 2 runs its cleanupIt deletes plans significantly older than the one it applied — and the stale plan Enactor 1 just activated is now the oldest.
  6. All endpoint IPs are deleteddynamodb.us-east-1.amazonaws.com now resolves to an empty record. Anything that can't find DynamoDB can't start.
  7. No Enactor can repair itThe system is in an inconsistent state where every automation instance fails to apply a plan. This is not a failure mode the automation knows how to handle.
  8. Manual operator interventionBecause the automation can't self-heal, a human has to repair the state. That's the moment a regional config bug becomes a regional outage.

The recovery, minute by minute

  1. 11:48 PM PDT · OCT 19
    DNS failures begin
    Every system in us-east-1 needing DynamoDB via the public endpoint fails to resolve it. 23 minutes until AWS's first public status update.
  2. 12:38 AM · OCT 20
    Root cause identified
    50 minutes in, engineers identify DynamoDB's DNS state as the source.
  3. 1:15 AM
    Temporary mitigations applied
    Workarounds let some internal services reach DynamoDB and repair key internal tooling, unblocking further recovery.
  4. 2:25 AM → 2:40 AM
    DNS fully restored
    All DNS information restored, global table replicas caught up by 2:32, and customers reconnect as cached records expire between 2:25 and 2:40.
  5. 4:14 AM → 5:28 AM
    DWFM host restarts
    Selective restarts clear processing queues so lease re-establishment can succeed across the EC2 fleet.
  6. 9:36 AM
    NLB health-check failover disabled
    Health checks were failing because new instances came into service before their network state propagated — taking healthy capacity out of service. Disabling failover brought those nodes back.
  7. 11:23 AM → 1:50 PM
    Throttles removed gradually
    Request throttles that protected the service during the pile-up are eased back one step at a time, watching for stability.
  8. 2:09 PM → 2:20 PM
    Resolved
    With EC2 stable, automatic health-check failover is re-enabled and all services return to normal.
What AWS changed afterwards: the DNS Planner and Enactor automation was disabled worldwide until the race condition was fixed and protections against incorrect DNS plans were added. NLB gained a velocity control so one load balancer can't shed that much capacity on health-check failover. EC2 gained a test suite for the DWFM recovery workflow, and its data-propagation throttling became queue-size aware.

Why they fail

This is the bit that should change your design priorities. 55% of major outages start with a change or code, and only 18% with power or cooling — the hardware story is the minority case.

Two structural causes sit underneath all of these. Shared control planes turn a zone event into a region event. Failed automation turns a recoverable fault into an incident needing a human.

The recurring pattern

Every incident on this page falls into one of four buckets:

  • A change went wrong — an incomplete allow list published region-wide, a malformed certificate pushed to every zone at once, metadata written by two control-plane versions, a router upgrade during which a technician unplugged every fiber path in 13 minutes.
  • Automation had a latent path — DNS planner races, Kinesis cell management, thundering-herd retry storms, expedited rollouts that skip validation, a PLC that sensed voltage upstream of its own breaker.
  • Physical infrastructure — lightning, transformers, arc flashes in power distribution units, cooling water leaks, a third-party data centre fire.
  • Shared dependencies — one regional control plane serving every zone, one DNS endpoint for a whole region, one global edge fleet behind one config pipeline.

The SLA maths: what the credit is actually worth

All three providers promise 99.99% for multi-zone compute. That's a real commitment. The remedy attached to it is a percentage of your bill — and that's where the maths gets uncomfortable.

Note the shape of the tiers. Zonal vs regional is the gap that matters — a single instance or a zonal-only deployment sits three orders of magnitude below the multi-zone promise. Architecture decides which number you can actually claim.

The credit doesn't cover the outage

Take the AWS us-east-1 event of October 2025: 15 hours of downtime in a 720-hour month is 97.9% uptime, which lands in the "below 99.0%" tier — a 30% service credit on your EC2 charges.

On a $5,000/month EC2 bill that's a $1,500 credit, applied to future invoices, which you have to claim manually within two billing cycles.

New Relic's 2026 Observability Forecast puts the average high-impact business outage at $1.85M per hour — roughly $27.7M over those 15 hours. Parametrix estimated the event at $500M–$650M for US companies.

The credit covers roughly 1/18,500th of the loss. SLA credits are a service gesture, not insurance. Model your outage cost separately, or budget it as uninsured risk.

The playbook: turning this into an SLO practice

What to actually measure, what to target, and what to do when the budget runs down. This is the operational translation of everything above.

SLIs worth measuring

Service typeSLITypical target
API / checkoutvalid requests served <400ms99.9%
Async job pipelinejobs completed within SLA99.0%
Data freshnessstaleness under threshold99.0%
Correctnessrequests without wrong results99.9%
Durationsuccessful requests in window99.9%
Throughputrequests served vs. demanded99.5%

Pick one SLI per user journey, not one per service. A checkout SLO that crosses frontend, API, database and payments is the one worth a budget.

Choosing the target

Set the SLO from what users need, then check it survives your architecture:

  1. Start from the customer-facing promise, then subtract. 99.9% customer-facing → 99.95% internal, if you want to absorb a dependency failure.
  2. Multiply out your dependency chain. If the composed number falls below your target, the architecture is the problem, not the target.
  3. Check the budget is real. 99.9% over 28 days = 40.3 minutes. If your release process can't reliably beat 40 minutes of badness a month, don't promise 99.9%.
  4. Set the window to match how fast you can respond. A 28-day window suits slow tickets; a 1-hour window suits a team that can deploy in minutes.
99.9% → 43.2 min/mo → 8.76 h/yr
99.95% → 21.6 min/mo → 4.38 h/yr
99.99% → 4.32 min/mo → 52.6 min/yr
Do
  • Freeze risky releases when the budget is exhausted — that's the entire point of having one.
  • Alert on burn rate, not on raw error rate. Error-rate alerts fire constantly and mean nothing.
  • Run active-active across two regions, not warm standby. A standby that never serves traffic is untested until the outage.
  • Remove single-region dependencies: identity, DNS, secrets, CI/CD and your observability stack are all common ones.
  • Failover automatically, with health-checked routing and safeguards so automation can't shed too much capacity at once — exactly the failure AWS hit in NLB.
  • Test failover continuously, and test the shared dependencies your failover path relies on.
Don't
  • Don't treat multi-AZ inside one region as regional resilience — every zone can share one control plane.
  • Don't promise a customer SLA you haven't actually composed. Multiply the layers first.
  • Don't count SLA credits as covering customer-facing penalties. They don't, and they never will.
  • Don't assume provider advice mid-incident is right. Microsoft's own East US 2 review called its failover advice "an inappropriate communication" — verify against your own user path.
  • Don't let the backup snapshot be written by the same pipeline that protects it. Azure's LKG snapshot was corrupted by the very change it existed to roll back.
  • Don't assume healthy replicas are spread across zones. GCP's Apigee had two of three datastore replicas in the zone that lost power, by scheduling misconfiguration.
09

Four conclusions that come straight out of the data

These are the ones worth memorising before an interview or a design review.

Multi-AZ is not regional resilience

In October 2025, teams that had spread replicas across availability zones still went down — every zone in us-east-1 depends on the same regional endpoint. Google made the point explicitly after europe-west2: "Google Cloud designs regional services to be robust to zone failures, but in this case there were a set of unexpected failures which caused impact outside of the zonal failure domain." Cross-region is the only answer to a regional control-plane failure.

Recovery, not detection, is what takes the hours

AWS identified the DynamoDB DNS root cause in 50 minutes and restored DNS by 2:25 AM — but the outage ran until 2:20 PM. The remaining twelve hours were throttles, stale leases, queue backlogs and false health checks. GCP's London recovery was the same shape: power restored within an hour, full service availability four hours later, because storage devices with unclean shutdowns had to be recovered before traffic was re-admitted. Plan your recovery, not just your detection.

Automation needs a way to fail safely, and a way to heal itself

DynamoDB's automation couldn't repair an empty-record state and needed a human. GCP's PLC logic couldn't see a dead feeder because it sampled upstream of its own breaker, so the generators never started. GCP's fix included validating PLC configurations at every site with similar topology, and making emergency operating procedures practised twice per shift. If your automation can produce a state it can't escape, that state is your outage.

The budget is the decision-making tool

99.99% is 4.32 minutes a month — one bad deploy. A 15-hour regional outage is 17× the entire annual allowance, and Azure's worst was 57×. Track provider-caused downtime in the same error-budget ledger as your own changes; when the provider burns your budget, you should find out before the invoice and the SLA claim do. An SLO isn't a target, it's a decision procedure.
