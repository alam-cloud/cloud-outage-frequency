How often do AWS and Azure regions and zones actually fall over?

Short answer: two to three times a year each, at a scale that lasts hours — not seconds. And critically, spreading across availability zones inside one region does not protect you. Both providers have had outages where every zone in a region died together because they shared one regional control plane.

Why they fail

This is the bit that should change your design priorities: the majority of major cloud outages are caused by software and change management, not by hardware dying.

The pattern across every incident

Look at the root causes in the timeline above and they cluster into four buckets:

  • A change went wrong — an incomplete allow list, a malformed cert, a routing removal, metadata written across two control-plane versions.
  • Automation had a latent path — DNS planner races, Kinesis cell management, thundering-herd retry storms, expedited rollouts that skip validation.
  • Physical infrastructure — lightning, transformers, cooling loops, generators that didn't take over when they should.
  • Shared dependencies — one regional control plane serving every zone in the region, which is how a "zone" event becomes a "region" event.

The SLA maths — what the credit is actually worth

Both providers promise 99.99% for multi-AZ compute. That is a real commitment. But the remedy attached to it is a percentage of your bill, and that's where the maths gets uncomfortable.

Note the AWS single-instance tier is 99.5% and Azure's single-VM tier varies by disk class (99.9% Premium / 99.5% Standard SSD / 95% Standard HDD). Architecture decides which SLA you can actually claim.

The credit doesn't cover the outage

Take the AWS us-east-1 event of October 2025: 15 hours of downtime in a 720-hour month is 97.9% uptime, which lands in the "below 99.0%" tier — a 30% service credit on your EC2 charges.

On a $5,000/month EC2 bill that's a $1,500 credit, applied to future invoices, which you have to claim manually within two billing cycles.

Meanwhile New Relic's 2026 Observability Forecast puts the average high-impact business outage at $1.85M per hour — roughly $27.7M over those 15 hours.

The credit covers roughly 1/18,500th of the loss. SLA credits are a service gesture, not insurance. Model your outage cost separately or budget it as uninsured risk.

06

What this means for your architecture

Four conclusions that come straight out of the incident data.

Multi-AZ inside one region is not regional resilience

In October 2025, teams that had spread replicas across availability zones still went down — every zone in us-east-1 depended on the same regional endpoint. And in Azure's 2025 multi-region events, the Uptime Institute found that even where regional failover had been planned, application downtime still occurred. Cross-region is the only real answer to a regional control-plane failure.

Failover has to be automatic, or it doesn't exist

A 50-hour recovery on Azure East US 2 was mostly the system failing to fail back cleanly, not the initial failure. If your runbook needs a human, a human is on holiday in week one of an outage. Test failover continuously, and test the DNS/control-plane dependencies your failover path shares with the thing it's failing over from.

Assume the shared dependency is the failure domain

DynamoDB's DNS taking down EC2 launches. Route 53's API down with the console. Front Door metadata crashing edge servers globally. The thing you depend on is probably a control plane inside the same region. Enumerate those dependencies explicitly — including your own DNS, your identity provider and your CI/CD — and treat each as a single point of failure.

Watch your error budget as a financial instrument

99.99% is 4.32 minutes a month — one bad deploy. A 15-hour regional outage is 17× the entire annual allowance. Track provider-caused downtime in the same error-budget ledger as your own changes; if the provider burns your budget, you should know before the invoice and the SLA claim do.
